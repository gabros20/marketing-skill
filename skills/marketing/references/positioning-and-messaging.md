# Positioning and Messaging

Purpose: Set how a product is framed against alternatives and translated into claims a specific
audience will believe — as well-regarded practitioner craft, not as an extension of the empirical
laws in [marketing-science.md](marketing-science.md). This is the pack's front-door job: the incumbent
skill-pack ecosystem has no dedicated positioning skill at all.

Read when:
- A product needs a positioning statement, a category claim, or a competitive frame before copy gets
  written.
- Messaging feels generic, feature-led, or fails a five-second read test.
- A repositioning is being considered and needs a rollout plan, not just a new tagline.

Skip when:
- The question is which empirical law applies and at what evidence tier →
  [marketing-science.md](marketing-science.md).
- The question is which segment or ICP stage to target, or a JTBD discovery job → [audience-and-
  segmentation.md](audience-and-segmentation.md).
- The question is landing-page requirements, proof placement, or conversion mechanics — a page turns
  positioning into a requirements brief; that brief lives in
  [landing-pages-and-conversion.md](landing-pages-and-conversion.md), and the page composition itself
  belongs to **design's surface-website product-narrative question chain (the 8 beats)** — cite it,
  never restate it here.
- The question is A/B testing a headline or running a message experiment → that crosses into `growth`.

Inputs: the product, its real alternatives (including "do nothing"), and at least one candidate
audience segment.
Produces: a positioning statement, a category and competitive frame, a messaging ladder, and — if
repositioning — a staged rollout with go/no-go checkpoints.

## Dunford's five components (craft, not science)

April Dunford's positioning process (*Obviously Awesome*; extended for sales narrative in *Sales
Pitch*) is the practitioner-canonical method, and she is explicit about its status: she describes
building it by trial and error across seven startups, influenced by jobs-to-be-done thinking, and
claims no empirical validation. No study testing whether the framework improves outcomes was found in
this pack's research. Teach it as widely-adopted, well-regarded craft — never dress it as science, and
never cite it to a compiler repo that reprints her framework; cite the original books or her own site.

Five components, worked in order:

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would the customer use or do if this didn't exist, including
   "nothing" or a manual workaround. This sets the comparison frame; you cannot position in a vacuum.
2. **Unique attributes** — what this product has or does that the alternatives don't.
3. **Value** — the proven benefit those attributes deliver, not the attributes themselves.
4. **Best-fit customers** — who cares most about that value; positioning is choosing who it's *for* as
   much as what it *is*.
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference the customer already understands ("Ries & Trout,
   *Positioning*," 1981: you position against what the prospect already knows, not into a void). An
   optional sixth component, a relevant market trend, can sharpen timing but isn't load-bearing.

Category design (creating a wholly new category rather than fitting an existing one) is high-variance
and case studies in that literature are survivorship-biased — treat it skeptically, as a deliberate,
high-risk choice, not a default.

## The positioning template

A compact way to force all five components into one sentence, worth using as a working draft rather
than final copy:

> For **[audience]**, **[product]** is the **[category]** that **[benefit]** because **[reason to
> believe]**.

Pair it with a battlecard: what the top alternatives get right, where this product wins, and the
objections a buyer will raise comparing the two. Comparison content belongs where the doubt actually
arises in a buyer's evaluation, not buried in an appendix — each comparison point should resolve
directly into the next action, not just sit as reference material.

## Category and competitive framing

Two distinct competitive situations get conflated and shouldn't be: a **brutally competitive market**
where entrenched competitors must be actively displaced, versus a market where the real contest is for
a buyer's discretionary attention or budget, not a head-to-head vendor fight. The framing — and the
GTM motion built on top of it — has to match which situation the product is actually in; treating both
as "competitive" the same way is a category error.

Two supporting techniques, independent of pricing (pricing tiers are `product`'s call, not marketing's
— this pack writes the comparison copy, not the tier structure):

- **Curation as positioning.** What gets shown (a portfolio, a feature list, a case-study set) is
  itself a positioning signal — a curated, selective set reads as confidence; an over-broad one dilutes
  the strongest claim.
- **Authority borrowing.** Third-party credibility markers (press mentions, certifications, named
  customers) support a claim instead of asserting it directly — the failure mode is "badge soup": one
  credible mark reads stronger than ten vague ones.

## Messaging architecture and laddering

One core claim per audience, then **ladder** it: feature → benefit → outcome. A feature dump assumes
the reader will do the translation themselves; most won't. Every claim should survive being read cold
in about five seconds — if the reader can't tell who this is for and what it does that fast, the copy
fails before anything else about it matters, and jargon that only an insider parses is the most common
way B2B copy fails that test.

Specificity beats superlatives: a named client, an exact number, a real outcome outperforms "leading"
or "best-in-class" every time — vague claims are also the ones a buyer discounts fastest.

## Copywriting frameworks, named and taught

These are stable, decades-old mnemonics for structuring a piece of copy — general craft, not a graded
empirical claim, so no evidence tier applies:

- **AIDA** — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action: the oldest structure, good for a single linear read
  (a landing page, a cold email).
- **PAS** — Problem, Agitate, Solution: name the problem, make its cost concrete, then resolve it —
  strong when the reader doesn't yet feel urgency.
- **BAB** — Before, After, Bridge: paint the current state, the desired state, then the product as the
  bridge between them.
- **PASTOR** — Problem, Amplify, Story, Transformation, Offer, Response: a longer-form variant of PAS
  for sales pages and launch narratives that need more room to build a case.

Choose one per piece rather than blending; a page that tries to be PAS and AIDA at once loses the
structural clarity either framework provides on its own.

## Message-testing reality: what crosses to growth

Positioning and messaging decisions can and should be validated cheaply before they're finalized: a
five-second test (can a fresh reader state who it's for and what it does), a squint test (does the
hierarchy still read with the copy blurred), or an informal read from outside the team. That is
message-market fit checking, and it belongs here.

The moment a message question becomes a controlled experiment — an A/B test on a headline, a
statistically-powered landing-page variant test, a multivariate CTA trial — it has crossed into
`growth`'s territory: experiment design, sample-size math, and readout are its job, not this pack's.
Marketing writes the candidate messages; growth decides how to test them at scale.

## The Crawl-Walk-Run repositioning rollout and defensibility hierarchy

Repositioning an existing product is riskier than positioning a new one — existing customers and
collateral already carry the old frame. A staged rollout with explicit go/no-go checkpoints, rather
than a single big-bang relaunch, is the safer default:

- **Crawl** — test the new frame in a low-stakes surface (sales conversations, a subset of ads, a
  single landing page) before touching primary brand assets.
- **Walk** — extend to a wider surface once the crawl stage clears its go/no-go threshold, while
  legacy messaging is still live elsewhere.
- **Run** — commit fully: primary site, sales collateral, and brand assets all carry the new frame.

Pair the rollout with a **defensibility hierarchy** for any competitive claim, strongest to weakest:
structural advantage (something a competitor cannot easily replicate) beats market position (share,
brand recognition) beats a product feature (copyable, usually fast). Before shipping a competitive
claim, ask which tier it actually sits on — a feature-level claim framed as if it were structural
overpromises and ages badly the moment a competitor ships the same feature.

(Crawl-Walk-Run rollout and defensibility hierarchy: `gtm-positioning-strategy`, `github/awesome-
copilot`, MIT, authored by Smit Patel — credited by name per the pack's licensing rule on quotable
MIT sources.)

## Validation
- [ ] The positioning statement names a real competitive alternative, not a strawman or "no one."
- [ ] Every claim in the messaging ladder resolves to an outcome, not just a feature.
- [ ] A competitive claim is checked against the defensibility hierarchy before it ships as durable.
- [ ] Dunford's method is presented as craft with no empirical-validation claim attached to it.
- [ ] A repositioning has a stated Crawl stage and a go/no-go threshold before Walk or Run.

## Failure modes and handoff
- The draft reads as a feature list with no ladder to an outcome — return to the laddering step before
  it goes further.
- A competitive or brand claim borrows [marketing-science.md](marketing-science.md)'s evidence tiers to
  sound more validated than Dunford's method actually is — positioning is craft; don't dress it as
  science.
- The real question is who the best-fit customer is, not how to frame the product for them →
  [audience-and-segmentation.md](audience-and-segmentation.md).
- The real question is how to prove a claim on a page (trust signals, objection placement) rather than
  what the claim should say → landing-pages-and-conversion.md.
- The messaging is ready to test at statistical scale, not just read-test → hand off to `growth`.
