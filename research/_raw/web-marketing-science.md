## 1. Double Jeopardy Law

### SOURCE: Ehrenberg, A.S.C. (1959) "The Pattern of Consumer Purchases," *Applied Statistics*; and Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise (1990) "Double Jeopardy Revisited," *Journal of Marketing* 54(3):82–91.
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** The original/canonical empirical basis: brands with smaller market share have both fewer buyers AND those buyers are less loyal (lower purchase frequency, higher defection) — a compounding "double" penalty, not a single one. Established and replicated across decades of repeat-purchase consumer panel data (mostly FMCG).
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** Full text not retrieved (paywalled JSTOR/Sage abstract only visible via search snippets). No quote taken — re-expressed above.
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** category-and-competitive-framing, positioning-and-messaging (sets realistic growth-share expectations; undercuts "loyalty-first" strategy framing for small/challenger brands).
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 (peer-reviewed, Journal of Marketing — top-tier). I could not access full text; found via search snippets and secondary citation only (ResearchGate/Academia.edu listing pages, not the paper itself). `UNVERIFIED: exact sample sizes/category count in the 1990 paper — would need paywalled JMR access or a university library`.

### SOURCE: Romaniuk, Dawes & Faghidno (May 2021), "The Double Jeopardy Law in B2B shows the way to grow," Ehrenberg-Bass Institute open-access report. https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/the-double-jeopardy-law-in-b2b-shows-the-way-to-grow
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** Extends double jeopardy to B2B categories: UK business banking, US business insurance (multiple lines), concrete suppliers, airplane buying, plus historical published studies (pharma prescriptions, aviation-fuel contracts, forest products, forex services) cited in the report's Table 4. Finding replicates: smaller B2B brands have both fewer customers and lower per-customer loyalty/higher defection. No B2B SaaS/subscription-software category is named anywhere in the dataset list.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** "loyalty is largely a predictable function of market share" (8 words) — Romaniuk, Dawes & Faghidno, 2021. "it is not possible to sustainably grow by focusing on loyalty alone" (12 words) — same source.
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** audience-and-segmentation, category-and-competitive-framing — directly usable as the evidentiary anchor for "penetration over loyalty" framing in B2B GTM.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute open-access report; institute is the field's leading empirical shop, but this is not itself a peer-reviewed journal article — it's a practitioner-facing report). No explicit sample sizes given; the report itself states only that "you need a large enough sample" without a number — `UNVERIFIED: exact n per B2B dataset`. As-of: report dated May 2021.

## 2. Duplication of Purchase Law

### SOURCE: Romaniuk, J. (May 2021), "How do B2B brands compete?", Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/how-do-b2b-brands-compete
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** Brands share customers proportional to competitors' market share (bigger competitors "steal" proportionally more of your customer base than smaller ones) — again originally a consumer-panel finding, here tested against three B2B datasets: US business insurance 2019 (16 products, State Farm/Allstate/Geico/Travelers/AIG/Humana, 93% correlation between sharing and penetration), historical B2B aviation-fuel contract data from Ehrenberg & Uncles (1990) — 30+ years old — and UK business banking 2019 (main-financial-institution switching over 12 months). No B2B SaaS/subscription dataset.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** "Growth will come from gaining more business customers from all other brands, proportionate with competitor share." (16 words)
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** category-and-competitive-framing — undercuts naive "steal share only from the closest competitor" positioning logic.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 (institute report, not peer-reviewed for the B2B extension specifically; the underlying law itself IS peer-reviewed consumer-panel literature). Report explicitly frames these as "observational patterns rather than controlled experiments" and flags the aviation-fuel data's age. As-of: May 2021.

## 3. Mental Availability, Physical Availability, Distinctive Assets

### SOURCE: Sharp, B. (2010), *How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know*, Oxford University Press. (Book; not open-access — I did not read full text, only secondary summaries/reviews.)
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** Synthesizes Ehrenberg-Bass panel-data findings into "mental availability" (propensity to be noticed/thought of in buying situations, built via distinctive brand assets: colors, logos, taglines, sonic cues) and "physical availability" (ease of purchase/distribution breadth) as the two drivers of brand growth, positioned as more important than differentiation. Original empirical base is FMCG/CPG repeat-purchase panel data across many countries.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none taken (book, not fetched).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** positioning-and-messaging ⭐, category-and-competitive-framing — this is the core citation practitioners lean on; pack should cite the underlying empirical reports (below) rather than restate the book's framing uncritically, given the live academic dispute (§7).
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 for the underlying panel-data findings it synthesizes; the book's own framing/rhetoric ("differentiation doesn't matter") is contested — see §7. `UNVERIFIED: I did not verify the book's own citations firsthand; assessment based on institute reports that post-date and extend it.`

### SOURCE: Nenycz-Thiel & Romaniuk (Nov 2025), "Easy to Find: Being Where B2B Buying Happens," Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/easy-to-find-being-where-b2b-buying-happens
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** Physical availability (ease of finding/buying) is presented as B2B's biggest growth lever, per secondary coverage in Marketing Week (fetch on marketingweek.com returned HTTP 403 — could not read directly). Title and framing corroborated by report link title itself.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none (source not fully readable).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** go-to-market-and-launch, seo-strategy (distribution/findability framing).
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 nominally (institute report), but I was blocked from the full text and from the Marketing Week secondary write-up (403 on both). `UNVERIFIED: full contents, dataset, sample size — would need direct access to the report PDF or a non-paywalled route.` As-of: November 2025 (very recent; flag for re-verification if pack ships materially later).

### SOURCE: Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, "Distinctive Asset Measurement" / "Identifying and Prioritising Category Entry Points" (commercial-research service pages, not open papers). https://marketingscience.info/learn-with-us/commercial-research/distinctive-asset
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** The institute sells a proprietary "distinctive asset grid" methodology (fame × uniqueness) as a commercial service — this is institute methodology, not a public dataset or published finding.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none (marketing page, not a research report).

## 4. Pareto ~60/40 (not 80/20)

### SOURCE: Sharp & Romaniuk (2007), "There is a Pareto Law – but not as you know it," Ehrenberg-Bass Institute sponsor report (original source, cited in *How Brands Grow*). Not independently fetched — found only via secondary description (marketingscience.info "The value of Pareto's bottom 80%" blog post and book summaries).
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** The commonly cited "80/20 rule" (80% of sales from 20% of buyers) does not hold empirically for most brands studied; instead a brand's heaviest 20% of buyers deliver roughly half of sales, and the lightest 80% of buyers deliver almost the other half — hence "60/40," not "80/20." Light/infrequent buyers matter far more to volume than folklore assumes.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none (original 2007 report not independently fetched — I have the finding only via secondary paraphrase).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** audience-and-segmentation ⭐ — directly supports a "don't over-index on power users/loyalists" framing for the pack.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2, but with a caveat: I could not open the 2007 original report, the SSRN abstract page (papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3498097, 403), or either ResearchGate/Academia.edu mirror (403) for the follow-up paper below. Everything here is secondary-sourced. `UNVERIFIED: exact category list and country count for the original 2007 study.`

### SOURCE: Sharp, Romaniuk & Graham, "Marketing's 60/20 Pareto Law" — listed on SSRN (abstract id 3498097), ResearchGate (publication 338094423), Academia.edu (103012328).
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** A later, more precisely-titled paper restating the same finding as "60/20" (top 20% of buyers ≈ 50–60% of sales, not 80%).
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none — abstract itself was not retrievable (all three mirrors returned HTTP 403 to WebFetch).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** audience-and-segmentation.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 by provenance (SSRN-hosted, named EB Institute authors) but **I only verified the paper's existence, title, and author list — not its abstract or findings directly**. `UNVERIFIED: full abstract, journal of record, exact figures — needs SSRN/ResearchGate account access.`

## 5. The 95-5 Rule

### SOURCE: Dawes, J. (May 2021), "Advertising effectiveness and the 95-5 rule: most B2B buyers are not in the market right now," Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (commissioned for LinkedIn B2B Institute). https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/advertising-effectiveness-and-the-95-5-rule-most-b2b-buyers-are-not-in-the-market-right-now
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** **Important finding: the "95%" figure is explicitly NOT a direct empirical measurement.** It is calculated/derived from average category interpurchase intervals (if buyers replace a service every ~5 years on average, then in any given period only a small fraction are "in-market"). No specific dataset, category list, or sample size is given in the report; only generic illustrative examples (phone systems, payroll software, carpet, "law firm/bank switched every ~5 years").
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** "The 95% figure is not meant to be a precise rule... using it as a heuristic to get the idea across" (22 words, elision within source's own sentence) — Dawes, 2021. "If you know the average interpurchase time for your category you can readily calculate the proportion of potential buyers who are in-market." (21 words)
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** go-to-market-and-launch, paid-acquisition, lifecycle-and-email — but the pack should NOT present "95%" as a measured constant; it is a category-agnostic heuristic derived from a formula, not a panel-data finding like double jeopardy.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 (institute-authored, LinkedIn B2B Institute-commissioned) for the *logic*, but rung 3–4 for the specific "95%" number, which the author himself disclaims as illustrative rather than precise. As-of May 2021. This is the single most over-cited/under-scrutinized number in the practitioner literature per this research — see closing section.

## 6. Binet & Field — Long/Short, 60/40, ESOV

### SOURCE: Binet, L. & Field, P. (2013), "The Long and the Short of It," IPA (Institute of Practitioners in Advertising), UK. Based on ~1,000 case studies submitted to the IPA Effectiveness Awards / held in the IPA Databank (1980–2013 submissions).
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** Optimal average split ≈ 60% brand-building (broad-reach, emotional, long-term memory-structure building) / 40% activation (targeted, short-term, promotional) for B2C. Brand-building compounds over time; activation produces sharp but short-lived sales uplift. Campaigns near 60/40 outperformed those tilted to either extreme, on effectiveness metrics reported by the submitting agencies/brands themselves.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none independently fetched (I only reached secondary summaries — alexmurrell.co.uk, growthmethod.com, ipa.co.uk listing page — not the PDF/report itself).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** go-to-market-and-launch, paid-acquisition, positioning-and-messaging (budget-split framing).
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 3, not rung 2 — **this is not peer-reviewed academic literature; it is an industry-body report built on a self-submitted awards-competition case-study corpus.** Selection/survivorship bias is structural: only agencies/brands who believe they won, and who choose to enter the (paid, effortful) awards process, appear in the databank; it skews toward larger advertisers with agency relationships and above-average measured outcomes (an awards corpus, almost by definition, excludes failures). One practitioner critique found in this research (Richard Shotton, re: a *different* IPA-databank analysis on "purpose" campaigns) makes the same structural point: subsetting the databank by an outcome-correlated criterion produces circular-seeming support for whatever thesis is being tested. This is a real, citable methodological concern for the whole IPA-databank literature, not just the purpose-campaign case.

### SOURCE: LinkedIn B2B Institute + Binet & Field (2019), "The Long and the Short of B2B Branding" (title as commonly cited; I did not reach a primary PDF — content triangulated from thedrum.com, linkedin.com/business blog posts, and a Medium critique).
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** When the IPA databank case studies are filtered to B2B-only submissions, the optimal split reported is **~46% brand-building / 54% activation** — i.e. reversed from B2C's 60/40, tilted toward activation. Rationale given: longer B2B sales cycles give activation media more time to convert; account-based targeting improves activation efficiency.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none — no primary-source quote obtained (all fetch attempts on primary LinkedIn B2B Institute research-hub pages were not made directly; content is search-snippet triangulated across 3 independent secondary write-ups that agree on the 46/54 figure, which raises confidence somewhat, but none is the primary document).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** go-to-market-and-launch, paid-acquisition — this is the number the pack should use if citing a B2B split, NOT the B2C 60/40 (a live confusion in the practitioner ecosystem, per a Medium post titled "Myth #1: 60:40 is a golden rule for B2B marketing budgets" found in this research, arguing marketers wrongly apply the B2C ratio to B2B).
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 3 (industry-body/vendor-commissioned research; LinkedIn B2B Institute is a vendor, and the case corpus is the same self-submitted IPA databank, now B2B-filtered, so the same selection-bias caveat applies plus a further B2B-subset-size caveat). `UNVERIFIED: I could not confirm the exact B2B sub-sample size (n of B2B case studies within the ~1,000-case databank) — likely small given B2B is a minority of IPA awards entries; would need the primary LinkedIn B2B Institute PDF.` As-of: 2019 finding, still the most-cited B2B figure as of this research (2026).

### SOURCE: Excess Share of Voice (ESOV) → share growth (Binet & Field, various IPA-era publications, commonly dated ~2007–2009).
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** Brands whose share of voice (advertising presence) exceeds their share of market tend to grow share; the reverse tends to decline.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none (conflicting secondary paraphrase only, no primary fetch).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** paid-acquisition, attribution-and-measurement.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 3–4 — **flag a live inconsistency**: one secondary source states "10-point ESOV increase → ~0.5% annual share growth," another states "10% ESOV → 0.7% share growth per year." I could not resolve which (if either) reflects the primary IPA analysis; this is exactly the kind of practitioner-telephone-game number the charter warns about. `UNVERIFIED: the correct coefficient and its confidence interval — needs the primary Binet & Field IPA paper, not found/fetched in this session.`

## 7. Category Entry Points (CEPs)

### SOURCE: Romaniuk, J. (2021), *Building Mental Availability: How Brands Grow, Part 2*, Oxford University Press; and Romaniuk & Sharp (2000), "Using known patterns in image data to determine brand positioning" (earliest foundational CEP paper, per report citation — not independently fetched).
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** CEPs are the situational/motivational cues ("thirsty," "hosting a party," "need X for an urgent client deadline") buyers use to retrieve brands from memory; brands grow by building associative links to as many relevant CEPs as possible, not by owning one positioning statement.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** "Category Entry Points (CEPs) are the cues that category buyers use to access their memories when faced with a buying situation" (21 words) — Romaniuk, cited via marketingscience.info B2B CEP report.
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** positioning-and-messaging, content-strategy-and-briefs, seo-strategy (CEPs map naturally onto search-intent clusters).
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 (book + institute-cited foundational paper), but I did not independently fetch the 2000 paper or the 2021 book — relying on the B2B report's citation of them.

### SOURCE: Romaniuk, J. (July 2022), "Category Entry Points in a Business-to-Business (B2B) World," Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/category-entry-points-in-a-business-to-business-b2b-world
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** Tests CEP theory against three B2B datasets: US business insurance (606 customers, 17 products), US online-advertising decision-makers (311, a LinkedIn Marketing Solutions-run study), UK business banking (609 customers). Across 16 of 17 insurance products, each additional CEP linked to a brand in a customer's memory reduced defection probability by ~5%.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** "for each additional CEP a customer has linked to the brand in memory, probability of defection lowers by 5%" (19 words).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** positioning-and-messaging ⭐, content-strategy-and-briefs — this is the best-evidenced B2B-specific finding in this entire research pass: real sample sizes (300–600+ respondents per dataset), a named co-sponsor (LinkedIn Marketing Solutions), and a consistent effect across 16/17 products tested.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2, and among the strongest sources found — actual sample sizes disclosed (rare in this literature), specific per-dataset results, and an honest caveat quoted from the report itself: predictive scales are imperfect ("not everyone with a high probability of defection actually switches... some people with a low probability still switch"). As-of July 2022. No SaaS/subscription-software dataset present.

## 8. Critiques and limits of Sharp / Ehrenberg-Bass

### SOURCE: Felipe Thomaz (Associate Professor of Marketing, Saïd Business School, Oxford), quoted in Contagious.com, "'Byron Sharp is ignoring 60 years of published work'." https://www.contagious.com/en/article/news-and-views/byron-sharp-how-brands-grow-ignores-60-years-of-published-work-felipe-thomaz
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** A named, credentialed academic critique (not an anonymous blog take): Thomaz argues Sharp built his framework by taking Keller's 1993 brand-equity model and stripping out differentiation, and that "mental/physical availability" are Sharp's own popularization terms, not established marketing-science vocabulary most academics recognize. He also argues Ehrenberg's original work described static markets, not growth mechanics, so using it to prescribe how to grow a brand is a category error.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** "by removing differentiation, he's broken branding" (6 words). "Those ideas don't exist in marketing science... most academics have never heard of [them]" (paraphrase-elided to stay under cap; original ~21 words per source fetch).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** positioning-and-messaging — this is the load-bearing counter-citation the pack needs so it doesn't present "differentiation doesn't matter" as settled fact.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2-adjacent (named tenured academic at a top business school, quoted directly by a trade publication; not itself a peer-reviewed rebuttal paper, so treat as a credible expert opinion rather than a citable study). `UNVERIFIED: whether Thomaz has published a formal peer-reviewed rebuttal — not found in this search pass.`

### SOURCE: Romaniuk, Sharp & Ehrenberg (2007), "Evidence concerning the Importance of Perceived Brand Differentiation," *Australasian Marketing Journal* 15(2):42–54. PDF: https://www.marketingscience.info/wp-content/uploads/staff/2015/08/different.pdf (fetch returned 404 in this session; citation and abstract triangulated via ScienceDirect/Semantic Scholar/Sage listing pages).
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** The institute's own peer-reviewed evidence for the "differentiation is overrated" claim: across many categories and two countries, perceived brand differentiation between competing brands is low, yet customers still buy differently-shared amounts from each — implying differentiation is not doing the explanatory work classic branding theory assigns it. Also argues standard multivariate perceptual-mapping techniques overstate small differences.
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none obtained (PDF link 404'd; only abstract-level paraphrase available from listing pages).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** positioning-and-messaging — this is the primary peer-reviewed article underlying the "differentiation doesn't matter" claim; important that it IS peer-reviewed (Rung 2), unlike the IPA/Binet-Field material.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** Rung 2 (peer-reviewed, Australasian Marketing Journal), but note: authored by Sharp/Romaniuk/Ehrenberg themselves — i.e. the claim's originators publishing the evidence for their own claim, in a journal (AMJ) that is not top-tier by international standards. Not independent replication. Sits opposite Thomaz's critique above — present both.

### SOURCE: "Empirical Generalisation in Marketing" debate re: High-Involvement Products and Services (HIPS), ScienceDirect abstract (S1441358204701020) — full text not fetched, found via search snippet only.
**WHAT IT TEACHES:** A specific, on-point academic argument that empirical-generalization methodology (the whole Ehrenberg-Bass paradigm) may be less applicable to High-Involvement Products and Services than to the low-involvement, frequently-repurchased FMCG categories where it was developed. This is directly relevant to B2B SaaS (typically higher-involvement, longer sales cycle, lower purchase frequency than the panel categories the laws were built on).
**VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING:** none (abstract-level snippet only).
**RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS:** category-and-competitive-framing, positioning-and-messaging — this is the single most directly relevant piece of "does this transfer to our domain" academic pushback found in this research.
**CREDIBILITY NOTE:** `UNVERIFIED: author(s), full argument, and year` — I have only a ScienceDirect abstract ID and a paraphrased snippet from a web-search summarizer, not the paper itself. Flagging because the finding-shape is important even though the citation is thin; a sub-orchestrator or later pass should track down the full paper before citing it as a source rather than a lead.

## Where the evidence ends

**Target 1 — Ehrenberg-Bass laws (double jeopardy, duplication of purchase, mental/physical availability, Pareto 60/40, penetration-over-loyalty):**
(a) **Well-evidenced in original domain.** Decades of replication across FMCG/CPG repeat-purchase consumer panel data, many countries, peer-reviewed (double jeopardy, duplication of purchase, Pareto). This is the strongest empirical base in all of marketing.
(b) **Plausibly transferable to B2B — actively being tested, not yet as deep.** The institute has run B2B replications (banking, insurance, aviation fuel, concrete, airplanes) with real if modest datasets, and the direction of effect replicates. **No B2B SaaS/subscription-software category appears in any dataset I found.** Treat "this applies to SaaS" as a plausible extrapolation the institute itself hasn't published, not a tested finding.
(c) **Folklore extrapolation.** Any claim that these laws describe zero-audience, pre-launch, n=1 founders. All underlying data requires an existing, measurable buyer population with repeat-purchase or panel behavior — structurally the opposite of a zero-audience startup. No paper found (in EB-Institute output or adjacent entrepreneurship journals) tests these laws at that stage.

**Target 2 — The 95-5 rule:**
(a)/(b) collapse together here: it is **not an independently measured empirical finding** even in its home B2B domain — it's a heuristic calculation from assumed average interpurchase intervals, explicitly disclaimed as imprecise by its own author (Dawes, 2021). (c) Presenting "95%" as a precise, universal, measured constant — which is how it circulates in practitioner content — is folklore-level precision-washing of a back-of-envelope estimate.

**Target 3 — Binet & Field / 60:40 / ESOV:**
(a) Well-evidenced *within its own corpus* (IPA Databank, ~1,000 award-submitted UK case studies) but that corpus itself has a structural selection bias (self-submitted, agency-mediated, award-seeking, therefore skewed toward large-budget advertisers with agency relationships and self-reported "wins" — not a random or even representative sample of all advertising). (b) The B2B revision (46:54, LinkedIn B2B Institute + Binet & Field, 2019) is a real, distinct, more-cited-than-verified number — I could not reach a primary document, only three convergent secondary summaries. The Medium post found in this research explicitly calls treating either ratio as a "golden rule" a myth. (c) Applying 60:40 (the B2C number) to B2B, which the same secondary sources say practitioners routinely do, is the folklore case in point — the corrected 46:54 exists precisely to correct that error. The ESOV→growth coefficient (0.5% vs 0.7% per 10pt ESOV, conflicting sources) is currently unverifiable to a single number.

**Target 4 — Category Entry Points:** (a)/(b) — this is the best-evidenced B2B-specific finding in the whole research pass: real disclosed sample sizes (300–600+ per dataset), a named third-party co-sponsor (LinkedIn Marketing Solutions), consistent direction across 16/17 tested products. Still no SaaS-specific dataset, but of everything reviewed this is the safest to cite as "tested in B2B, not just FMCG."

**Target 5 — Critiques:** A real, live, named-academic dispute exists (Thomaz vs. Sharp/Romaniuk/Ehrenberg), plus a directly-on-point methodological argument (HIPS/high-involvement categories may not fit the empirical-generalization paradigm — thin citation, flagged UNVERIFIED) and a structural critique of the IPA databank's selection bias. A pack that presents Sharp's laws as uncontested settled science would be wrong; a pack that presents them as unevidenced folklore would also be wrong. The honest position is: strong empirical base in FMCG, real but thinner and B2B-only-so-far extensions, contested framing claims (esp. "differentiation doesn't matter"), and a complete absence of evidence — for or against — at the zero-audience/n=1 startup stage, which is simply outside every dataset surveyed.

---

**Biggest surprise:** The 95-5 rule — probably the single most-quoted "statistic" in B2B marketing content — is not a measured empirical finding at all; it's an arithmetic heuristic from assumed interpurchase intervals, and the author says so in the primary source itself. Almost no secondary/practitioner content repeats that caveat.

**Could not verify:** exact sample sizes for the original double-jeopardy studies; full text/abstract of the "60/20 Pareto Law" SSRN paper (403 on all 3 mirrors); the primary LinkedIn B2B Institute 46:54 PDF (secondary-triangulated only); the correct ESOV→growth coefficient (conflicting secondary figures); the HIPS empirical-generalization critique's author/year (snippet only); whether Thomaz has a formal peer-reviewed rebuttal paper (news-quote only). All marked `UNVERIFIED:` inline above with what would resolve them.
