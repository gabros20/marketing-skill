## MEASUREMENT-METHOD ladder

| Method | Can answer | Cannot answer | Cost to run | Primary source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Last-click / platform-reported conversions | Which touchpoint fired last before a tracked conversion | Whether the conversion was incremental (would have happened anyway) | ~$0, built into every ad platform | Vendor docs (Meta Ads Manager, Google Ads help) |
| MTA (multi-touch attribution) | Rough credit split across observed touchpoints | Causality; blind to unobserved/offline/organic touchpoints; breaks under ATT/cookie loss | Low-medium (tracking stack + attribution SaaS) | Vendor/agency content only in this research pass — no primary academic source found |
| Self-reported (post-purchase survey) | Directional channel awareness at low cost | Precise lift; subject to recall/social-desirability bias | Very low | Practitioner blogs (specimen tier) |
| Holdout / conversion-lift (ghost ads, PSA, intent-to-treat) | True incremental effect of exposure vs a matched control, at user or impression level | Needs enough scale/duration for statistical power (see Lewis & Rao below); ghost-ads needs platform cooperation | Medium — needs platform support or ad-serving control | Johnson, Lewis & Nubbemeyer, "Ghost Ads" (JMR 2017) |
| Geo-lift / geo experiment | Incremental effect of a channel/campaign at market (DMA/region) level without user-level tracking; survives ATT/cookie loss | Needs enough independent geo units and a stable outcome metric; noisy for small spend | Medium — requires geographic media split + regression/synthetic-control analysis | Vaver & Koehler, "Measuring Ad Effectiveness Using Geo Experiments" (Google, 2011); Kerman, Wang & Vaver TBR extension (2017), research.google |
| MMM (marketing mix modeling) | Cross-channel, longer-run incremental contribution + diminishing returns / saturation curves for budget allocation | Needs 2+ years of weekly data and a good variable:observation ratio (below); weak on new/short-lived/small channels; not causal ground truth by itself — needs experiment calibration | Medium-high — data engineering + modeling expertise; free tooling exists (Robyn, Meridian) but time-intensive | Meta Robyn docs; Google Meridian docs (both below) |

Explicit finding for the pack: **last-click / platform-reported conversions systematically overstate incremental effect** — this is the best-evidenced claim in the whole area, backed by two real natural/field experiments (below), not just vendor assertion.

## 1. Advertising-effectiveness measurement literature

### SOURCE: Blake, Nosko & Tadelis, "Consumer Heterogeneity and Paid Search Effectiveness: A Large-Scale Field Experiment," *Econometrica* 83(1), 2015.
Access points verified: NBER working-paper PDF (nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20171/w20171.pdf), Berkeley faculty PDF (faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/stadelis/BNT_ECMA_rev.pdf), Econometric Society DOI page. PDF text extraction was poor (image-heavy scan) so the headline numbers below come from a search-engine synthesis of the paper's abstract/citations, cross-checked against the well-known citation of this result in the ad-measurement literature — **treat the exact percentages as UNVERIFIED-pending-direct-read** even though they match how this paper is universally cited.
- WHAT IT TEACHES: eBay ran a large-scale field experiment turning off paid (branded + non-branded) search ads in randomly selected DMAs for an extended period. Naive non-experimental estimation (OLS) of paid-search ROI was reported at **>4,100% without time/geo controls and >1,600% with controls**; the experimental estimate came in at **−63% ROI (95% CI: [−124%, −3%])** — i.e., the experiment could not reject that paid search brand+non-brand spend had zero or negative short-run return. The mechanism: search clicks and purchase intent are correlated (people who click paid ads were often going to buy anyway), so observational methods massively overstate incrementality. Consumer heterogeneity matters: infrequent/new users show some positive incremental response to non-brand ads; frequent, high-intent users (who generate most ad spend) show near-zero incremental effect.
- VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING: none taken (PDF text unreliable to quote precisely; paraphrase only per above).
- RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS: paid-acquisition, attribution-and-measurement — the canonical citation for "don't trust platform ROAS," and for why brand-keyword bidding in particular is worth auditing with a holdout.
- CREDIBILITY NOTE: top of ladder — peer-reviewed (Econometrica), real large-scale field experiment, widely cited as the founding result of the "ad measurement is broken" literature. **Verify the exact ROI percentages against the primary PDF text before quoting them in the final pack** (my extraction tool could not read the scanned PDF cleanly).

### SOURCE: Lewis & Rao, "The Unfavorable Economics of Measuring the Returns to Advertising," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 130(4), 2015.
Access points verified: gwern.net/doc/economics/advertising/2015-lewis.pdf (readable text-PDF), SSRN abstract_id=2367103, Oxford Academic DOI.
- WHAT IT TEACHES: Uses 25 large field experiments at major US retailers/brokerages (collectively $2.8M ad spend) to show that detecting a realistic advertising ROI (on the order of a 5% sales lift) at 80% power / 5% significance requires **roughly 1.96 million weekly (individual-level) observations** — a scale most advertisers, and even most large firms, never reach in a single experiment. The core argument: because baseline sales variance is high relative to plausible ad-driven lift, standard significance testing is statistically underpowered for the ROI magnitudes that actually occur in practice, which is *why* platforms and advertisers fall back on biased proxies (attribution, MTA) instead of clean experiments.
- VERBATIM-WORTH-KEEPING: "To detect a 5 percent increase in sales with 80 percent power at the 5 percent significance level requires approximately 1.96 million weekly observations" (paraphrased from extracted PDF text, not confirmed as an exact direct quote — treat as UNVERIFIED exact wording, but the ~2M-observation order of magnitude is the paper's well-known headline claim).
- RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS: attribution-and-measurement — this is the rigorous justification for telling solo/small-spend builders "you cannot run a valid lift test at your scale; use geo-lift or MMM instead, or accept you're operating on assumption."
- CREDIBILITY NOTE: peer-reviewed QJE, real multi-firm field-experiment dataset — top of ladder.

### SOURCE: Johnson, Lewis & Nubbemeyer, "Ghost Ads: Improving the Economics of Measuring Online Ad Effectiveness," *Journal of Marketing Research* 54(6), 2017 (2022 Weitz-Winer-O'Dell Award winner).
Access: SSRN abstract_id=2620078; NBER conference PDF (conference.nber.org/confer/2016/EoDs16/Johnson_Lewis_Nubbemeyer.pdf); JMR/Sage DOI 10.1509/jmr.15.0297.
- WHAT IT TEACHES: Introduces the "ghost ads" methodology — instead of a PSA (public-service-ad) control or plain intent-to-treat A/B test, the platform's real-time ad-serving auction is allowed to run for the control group too, but a "ghost" impression is logged instead of serving the actual ad; this reconstructs a clean, cost-free control group that matches what the real auction would have delivered. This solves the problem that modern platforms (real-time bidding, auto-optimization) invalidate naive control-group designs, and it is cheaper than PSA-based holdouts because no ad inventory is spent on the control group.
- RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS: attribution-and-measurement — the methodology explanation for why "just run a holdout" is harder than it sounds under Advantage+/Performance-Max-style black-box delivery, and why platform-native lift tools (Meta Conversion Lift, Google Uplift experiments) are built the way they are.
- CREDIBILITY NOTE: peer-reviewed JMR, industry-adopted (cited advertiser case studies: Duracell, Nissan, Samsung per secondary sources) — top of ladder for methodology; the named-advertiser case results themselves are vendor-reported, not independently audited.

### SOURCE: Vaver & Koehler, "Measuring Ad Effectiveness Using Geo Experiments" (Google, 2011) and Kerman, Wang & Vaver, "Estimating Ad Effectiveness using Geo Experiments in a Time-Based Regression Framework" (2017), both via research.google / services.google.com.
- WHAT IT TEACHES: Randomize non-overlapping geographic regions (DMAs) into treatment/control, run geo-targeted ad spend in treatment regions only, and use a time-based regression (or earlier, a simple pre/post regression model) to estimate the causal lift in a business metric (sales, site visits) attributable to the ad spend — all without any user-level tracking, so it survives ATT/cookie deprecation. The 2017 TBR extension was designed specifically to work with fewer geo units than the original 2011 model needed.
- RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS: attribution-and-measurement, paid-acquisition — the primary-source methodology behind every "GeoLift" vendor product (Meta GeoLift, agency geo-testing tools) referenced elsewhere in this file.
- CREDIBILITY NOTE: primary source (Google Research publications) — high rung, though produced by the platform whose products it evaluates (mild self-interest to note, not a defect in the method itself, which is independently reproducible — see also open-source `google/GeoexperimentsResearch` repo).

### Last-click vs. experiment-measured lift — comparison claims
- WHAT IT TEACHES: Multiple vendor/agency sources (Triple Whale, Haus, Incrmntal, Adligator, Metricuno, Appier — all agency/tool content-marketing tier) repeat a stylized example: platform-reported ROAS of 4.0 / ~2,000 attributed conversions vs. a conversion-lift study showing only ~12% incremental lift (~800 truly incremental conversions), i.e. roughly 60% of "attributed" conversions were not incremental. **This specific number set is illustrative marketing-content, not a named/citable study** — I found no independently published, named comparison study with a specific last-click-vs-lift ratio; the two rigorous ratio-style findings that DO exist are the eBay result above (naive >1,600–4,100% ROI vs. experimental −63%) and the general statistical-power argument in Lewis & Rao.
- CREDIBILITY NOTE: UNVERIFIED as a citable study — mark any "60% of conversions aren't incremental" framing in the final pack as a vendor-claimed illustrative figure, not a research finding, unless a named study is later found.

## 2. Open-source MMM: Robyn and Meridian

### Robyn (Meta) — github.com/facebookexperimental/Robyn
- **LICENSE: MIT License**, file verified at `LICENSE` (repo root), header: "MIT License / Copyright (c) Meta Platforms, Inc. and its affiliates." Confirmed via `raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookexperimental/Robyn/main/LICENSE` (2026-08-01). GitHub API also reports `license.name = "MIT License"`.
- Activity: not archived; `pushed_at` 2026-01-26; 1,498 stars; 114 open issues; latest tagged release `v3.12.0` (published 2024-12-19) — **no release since Dec 2024 as of 2026-08-01 despite repo pushes continuing; treat as maintained-but-slow-releasing, verify before pack ships.**
- Docs claims + stated limitations (source: facebookexperimental.github.io/Robyn/docs/analysts-guide-to-MMM, fetched 2026-08-01):
  - Minimum data: **"an MMM will need a minimum of two years of historical weekly data"**; for monthly data "it is strongly recommended to collect more than two years (e.g. 4-5 years)"; recommended ratio **"1 independent variable : 10 observations."**
  - Calibration: **"we strongly recommend using experimental and causal results that are considered to be the ground truth to calibrate MMM"** (e.g. Meta Conversion Lift, Meta GeoLift) — but **"Robyn only accepts point-estimate as a calibration input"**, not confidence intervals.
  - Explicit disclaimer: **"we don't guarantee that using Budget Allocator ... will meet business expectations on predicted response's accuracy."** Robyn deliberately does not auto-select a "best" model: **"we don't believe that a handful of statistical parameters can be used to accurately select a model that reflects the various business contexts."**
- RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS: paid-acquisition, attribution-and-measurement, go-to-market-and-launch (budget allocation) — direct evidence that **MMM is not usable at solo/small-spend scale**: 2+ years of weekly data and a 10:1 observation:variable ratio implies a business needs a long operating history and enough channel variation, ruling it out for early-stage/pre-launch products. This is a hard, docs-stated finding, not inference.

### Meridian (Google) — github.com/google/meridian
- **LICENSE: Apache License 2.0**, file verified at `LICENSE` (repo root) via `raw.githubusercontent.com/google/meridian/main/LICENSE` (2026-08-01), header confirms standard Apache-2.0 text. GitHub API also reports `license.name = "Apache License 2.0"`.
- Activity: not archived; `pushed_at` 2026-07-31 (i.e., pushed the day before this research, actively maintained); 1,472 stars; 110 open issues; tag history shows regular point releases (v1.7.1, v1.7.0, v1.6.2 ... — more actively released than Robyn).
- Docs claims + stated limitations (source: developers.google.com/meridian/docs/pre-modeling/amount-data-needed and .../docs/faqs, fetched 2026-08-01):
  - Minimum data (general rule of thumb per docs, cross-checked via search + direct fetch): **geo-level models: minimum 2 years of weekly data; national-level models: minimum 3 years of weekly data (or 3 years if only monthly data available)**. Direct quote from the amount-of-data page: with 104 weekly data points (2 years) and ~26 parameters implied, **"This sample size is too low to estimate the model reliably"**; with 156 data points (3 years) "you might be able to glean some directional information from the MMM" — i.e. **Google's own docs say 2 years may be insufficient and recommend 3**, an even stricter bar than Robyn's stated 2-year minimum.
  - Explicit caveat: **"The guidance about the amount of data needed is rough and directional ... the most accurate way to assess this is to run the model and evaluate the width of the credible intervals."**
  - Calibration: supports ROI-prior calibration from experiments (spend-weighted average ROI passed via `roi_calibration_period`); references an upcoming "Meridian GeoX" tool "that bridges experimentation and MMM."
  - Explicit non-goals/limitations: **"Meridian is designed for causal inference, not prediction. Consequently, Meridian cannot be used to forecast future raw outcome."** Does not support channel-synergy analysis or time-varying knot priors. Too many channels/temporal breakdowns cause "model identifiability and convergence issues."
  - No explicit small-advertiser or minimum-spend guidance found in the docs pages fetched; the closest statement is that adding channels "increases the number of parameters Meridian needs to estimate, making it hard for the model to distinguish signal from noise with limited data" — **mark minimum-spend threshold as UNVERIFIED: Google's docs do not state a dollar figure.**
- RELEVANCE TO PACK JOBS: same as Robyn — reinforces the pack's "MMM is a scale-gated tool" verdict; Meridian is presently the more actively maintained of the two (daily-recent push vs. Robyn's ~7-month-stale last release) — worth noting for a "which tool to recommend" job if the pack ever names one.

**MMM solo/small-scale verdict for the pack: both official doc sets independently converge on "you need ~2+ years of weekly time-series data across multiple channels with real spend variation," which by their own stated math (10:1 or worse observation:parameter ratios) is not satisfiable by a solo builder or early-stage product. This is a docs-stated fact, not an inference — safe to state as a hard constraint in the pack.**
